This paper introduces an augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR) interaction technology called "Hand Interfaces," which enables users to interact swiftly and effortlessly with a variety of virtual 3D objects within AR/VR environments by imitating these objects. The authors propose 28 distinct interaction designs centered around this technology and conduct two primary user studies. The findings affirm that Hand Interfaces are effective, expressive, and exceptionally enjoyable.

### Object Retrieval Tasks

The initial user study investigates the utilization of Hand Interfaces in object retrieval tasks, contrasting their performance with conventional drop-down menus and virtual grasp in terms of fidelity, mobility, and comfort. Several noteworthy observations emerge from this comparison:

1. The authors note the occurrence of the "uncanny valley" effect, exemplified by occlusion problems encountered when using virtual grasp, which can diminish the overall user experience.

2. Virtual grasp may be prone to ambiguity, where similar hand gestures are used for grabbing different objects, whereas Hand Interfaces offer an improved experience in this regard.

3. Employing Hand Interfaces leads to a more authentic experience, facilitating user immersion within the scenario and significantly enhancing the overall user experience.

### Interactive Control Tasks

The second study delves into the application of Hand Interfaces in interactive control tasks, yielding similar comparative results. It can be affirmed that Hand Interfaces deliver a more lifelike, enjoyable, and feedback-rich experience.

### Gesture Recognition

An additional intriguing aspect of this paper pertains to its gesture recognition for various manipulable and retrievable objects. It categorizes gestures into three modes: "no hands," "free hands," and "object retrieved." It employs a method involving the Euclidean space-weighted sum of skeletal coordinates (acquired from the Quest API) to deduce the current mode. Notably, once "object retrieved" is identified, users can interact with the object. To mitigate misrecognition during interaction due to changes in skeletal positions, the authors' team reduces the weighting of skeletal coordinates involved in the interaction, thereby diminishing misidentification and enhancing robustness.

For a more comprehensive understanding of the subject matter, please consult the [original paper](https://zhaosheng-thu.github.io/files/Hand%20Interface.pdf).
